## 19.1 The Question Behind the Chapter

Everything so far has, at most, considered a few processes on understood terms. This chapter confronts the world a staff engineer actually operates in: a *system of many programs, on many machines, communicating across a network* — a *distributed system*. And the chapter's central message, the thing it exists to deliver, is uncomfortable and important: **a distributed system is not a bigger program. It is a fundamentally different and harder kind of thing, and the difference is the network — an unreliable, slow, partition-prone medium that injects an entire catalog of hard problems that simply do not exist within one process.**

The mistake this chapter is written to prevent is the most common and most expensive mistake in modern systems engineering: treating a remote call as if it were a local call. Within a process, a function call is *fast*, it *always returns* (or raises, locally and immediately), and it either *happened or did not* — there is no third state. A call across the network has *none* of those properties. It is *slow* — milliseconds, not nanoseconds. It can *fail* — the network can drop it. It can *time out* — and a timeout tells you *nothing* about whether the operation happened. It can be *delayed arbitrarily*. The remote machine can be *down*, or — worse — *up but unreachable*, or *up but slow*. An engineer who carries the local-call mental model into a distributed system writes code that is wrong in ways that do not show up in testing and do show up, expensively, in production.

This chapter is the catalog of what the network forces on you, and the patterns for surviving it. It is also, with Chapters 12, 17, and 18, the heart of the system-design interview — the staff and principal system-design rounds *are*, very largely, a conversation about this chapter's material. Read it as the hardest and most defining body of knowledge in the technical half of the book.

## 19.2 The Fallacies of Distributed Computing

There is a famous list, decades old and still exactly correct, called the **Fallacies of Distributed Computing** — a set of false assumptions that engineers new to distributed systems reliably make. They are worth stating, because each fallacy, believed, produces a specific class of bug.

*The network is reliable.* It is not. Messages are lost. Connections drop. **Believing this** means writing code with no handling for a call that simply does not complete.

*Latency is zero.* It is not. A network round-trip is slow — milliseconds — and that is *enormous* next to a local operation. **Believing this** means designs that make many sequential remote calls and are therefore unacceptably slow, the distributed cousin of the N+1 problem.

*Bandwidth is infinite.* It is not. **Believing this** means shipping huge payloads across the network as though it were free.

*The network is secure.* It is not. **Believing this** means sending sensitive data unprotected (Chapter 22's territory).

*Topology doesn't change.* It does — machines come and go, addresses change, especially in modern cloud and container environments. **Believing this** means hard-coding the things that move.

*There is one administrator.* There is not — many systems, owned by many teams.

*Transport cost is zero.* It is not — serialization, network hardware, and operational overhead all cost.

*The network is homogeneous.* It is not — different machines, different versions, different behavior.

The fallacies are not a quiz to memorize; they are a *diagnostic*. When you find yourself, or a design, assuming that a remote call is fast, or reliable, or that the system's shape is stable, you have caught a fallacy in the act — and catching it is the first skill of distributed-systems work. The whole rest of the chapter is, in a sense, the set of patterns for *not* believing the fallacies.

## 19.3 The Shapes of Failure

Within one process, "failure" mostly means "an exception was raised" — and an exception is *informative*: it tells you, immediately and locally, that something went wrong and what. Distributed failure is different, and worse, and a staff engineer must know its three shapes.

**The crash.** A remote machine or process is *down* — it is not there. This is the *easiest* distributed failure, because it is at least somewhat detectable: a call to a crashed machine fails reasonably promptly (connection refused), and you know the machine is down.

**The network partition.** The remote machine is *up and running fine* — but you *cannot reach it*, because the network *between you* has failed. From your side, this is often indistinguishable from a crash: your calls fail or time out. But the remote machine is *not* down — it is alive, possibly still serving other clients that *can* reach it, possibly still modifying shared state. A partition means two halves of a system are *both alive but cannot communicate*, and may both believe the other is dead — and may both, therefore, take action as though they were the sole survivor. The partition is the failure that makes distributed systems genuinely hard, because it is the one where there is no single, agreed-upon truth about what is happening.

**The slow node — the worst one.** A remote machine is *up*, it is *reachable*, but it is *responding very slowly* — overloaded, struggling, degraded. This is the *most dangerous* distributed failure, and the reason is subtle and important: a slow node is *not detectable as a failure*. A crash fails fast and you know. A slow node *does not fail* — it just takes a long time — and your code, calling it, *waits*. And while your code waits on the slow node, *it holds resources* (Chapter 17's lesson: a worker, a connection, a task). A slow node does not announce itself; it quietly *consumes the resources of everything that calls it*, and it can drag down a whole healthy system by the simple mechanism of making everyone wait. The slow node is the failure mode that "fail fast or recover" systems handle and naive systems do not, and much of this chapter's patterns — timeouts, circuit breakers — exist specifically because the slow node is so dangerous and so undetectable.

The lesson of this section: distributed failure is not just "more failures." It is failures of a *different character* — failures that are *ambiguous* (a partition: is it down or just unreachable?), failures that are *silent* (a slow node: not a failure at all, just slowness, until it has consumed everything), failures where there is *no single truth*. Designing for distributed failure means designing for ambiguity and silence, not just for exceptions.

## 19.4 The Fundamental Trade-off: Consistency, Availability, and the CAP Theorem

Distributed systems face a trade-off so fundamental it has a theorem. The **CAP theorem** concerns three properties: **Consistency** (every read sees the most recent write — all parts of the system agree on the current data), **Availability** (every request gets a response — the system keeps working), and **Partition tolerance** (the system keeps functioning despite the network partitions of Section 19.3).

The theorem's statement, in the form a staff engineer should carry: **when a network partition occurs — and in any real distributed system, partitions *will* occur, so partition tolerance is not optional — you must choose between Consistency and Availability.** You cannot have both during a partition. The reason is intuitive once seen: a partition splits the system into halves that cannot communicate. A request arrives at one half, and it wants to write data. If the system insists on *Consistency*, that half cannot safely accept the write — it cannot coordinate with the other half, so accepting the write would risk the two halves disagreeing — so it must *refuse the request*, sacrificing *Availability*. If instead the system insists on *Availability*, that half *accepts* the write — but now the two halves have *diverged*, they *disagree* about the data, sacrificing *Consistency*. During a partition, you serve possibly-stale-or-conflicting data (choose Availability) or you refuse to serve (choose Consistency). There is no third option.

This is not an abstract puzzle; it is a *design decision* every distributed system makes, explicitly or by accident, and a staff engineer makes it *explicitly* and *per the needs of the data*. For some data, consistency is non-negotiable — a bank balance, an inventory count, anything where serving stale or conflicting data is a *correctness* failure — and for that data you choose Consistency and accept that during a partition the system refuses some requests. For other data, availability matters more and brief inconsistency is tolerable — a social feed, a view count, a recommendation — and for that data you choose Availability and accept that during a partition different users might see slightly different things. The CAP theorem does not tell you which to choose; it tells you that *you must choose*, that the choice depends on what the data *is*, and that a design which has not consciously made this choice has made it badly. (CAP is a simplification — real systems are more nuanced, and the related framing "PACELC" extends it — but the core lesson, that consistency and availability trade off and the trade is data-dependent, is the durable and essential one.)

This connects directly to Chapter 18's **eventual consistency**. A system that chooses Availability is typically *eventually consistent*: after a write, the different parts of the system *converge* on agreement *eventually* — quickly, usually — but for a window, a read might see stale data. Eventual consistency is not a flaw; it is a *deliberate choice* on the CAP trade-off, correct for data where availability matters more than instant agreement. The staff-level skill is knowing, for each piece of a system's data, *which* consistency model it needs and designing accordingly — strong consistency where correctness demands it, eventual consistency where availability is worth the staleness.

## 19.5 Communication: Messages and Brokers

How do the parts of a distributed system actually *talk*? Two broad styles, and a staff engineer should understand both and when each fits.

**Synchronous request/response** is the style of Chapter 17: one service calls another and *waits* for the response. It is direct, simple to reason about, and correct for "I need an answer now to continue." Its cost is *coupling in time*: the caller is blocked until the callee responds, and the caller's fate is tied to the callee's availability — if the callee is down or slow, the caller is directly affected (which is why Chapter 17's resilience patterns exist).

**Asynchronous messaging** decouples them. Instead of calling a service directly and waiting, a service *publishes a message* — "this thing happened," or "please do this work" — to a **message broker**, an intermediary, and goes on its way. Other services *consume* messages from the broker, on their own schedule. The publisher does not wait, does not know or care which services consume the message, and is not directly affected if a consumer is momentarily down — the broker holds the message until the consumer is ready. This is *temporal decoupling*, and it is powerful: it lets parts of a system absorb each other's slowness and downtime, it lets one event fan out to many consumers, and it smooths load spikes (the broker's queue buffers a burst). The cost is that the interaction is no longer immediate — the work happens *eventually* — and the system is now eventually consistent with respect to that work, and harder to trace end-to-end.

The major message brokers a staff engineer should know by name and character: **Kafka** is a high-throughput distributed *log* — messages are durably retained in ordered streams, and its model suits event streaming and high-volume pipelines where consumers may replay history. **RabbitMQ** is a traditional *message queue* broker with rich routing. **Redis** (Chapter 18) can also serve as a lightweight message channel. **Cloud-managed queues** — the major cloud providers each offer them — remove the operational burden of running a broker yourself. The choice among them is a real decision (throughput, durability, ordering guarantees, routing flexibility, operational cost), but the more fundamental decision is the one above it: *synchronous request/response or asynchronous messaging* — and that is decided by whether the caller genuinely needs an answer *now*, or whether the work can happen *eventually* with the benefit of temporal decoupling.

Closely related are **task queues** — systems for running background work, where a service hands off a unit of work to be done by separate *worker* processes, not in the request path. In the Python world, **Celery** is the long-established task queue; there are lighter modern alternatives. Task queues are the right tool for work that should not block a request: sending an email, generating a report, processing an upload. They are, in essence, asynchronous messaging applied to "do this work later."

## 19.6 Delivery Semantics and the Idempotency Mandate

When a message is sent across an unreliable network, a precise and important question arises: *how many times will it be delivered?* There are three possible answers — **delivery semantics** — and a staff engineer must understand them, because they determine how the consuming code must be written.

**At-most-once** delivery: a message is delivered *zero or one* times — it might be *lost*, but it will never be *duplicated*. This is what you get if the sender does not retry. It is simple, and acceptable only when losing a message occasionally is tolerable.

**At-least-once** delivery: a message is delivered *one or more* times — it will never be *lost*, but it *might be duplicated*. This is what you get when the sender *retries* until it gets an acknowledgment — and it *will* sometimes produce duplicates, because of exactly the ambiguity Section 19.3 described: the message is delivered and processed, but the *acknowledgment* is lost on the way back, so the sender, having received no ack, retries, and the message is delivered *again*. **At-least-once is the practical default** for systems that care about not losing messages, and it is what most real systems use.

**Exactly-once** delivery: a message is delivered *exactly* once — never lost, never duplicated. This is what everyone *wants*, and the hard truth a staff engineer must internalize is: **true exactly-once delivery is, in the general case, impossible** over an unreliable network — for the same fundamental reason the network is hard. The sender can never be *certain* whether a message arrived, so it must either risk loss (at-most-once) or risk duplication (at-least-once); there is no way to be certain of *exactly* one.

So if exactly-once delivery is impossible, and at-least-once (the sane default) produces duplicates, how does a real system process each message's *effect* exactly once? The answer is the single most important pattern in this chapter, and it is the **idempotency mandate**:

**Use at-least-once delivery, and make every consumer idempotent.** Idempotency was introduced in Chapter 17 — an operation whose effect is the same whether performed once or many times. If message *delivery* is at-least-once (so duplicates happen) but message *processing* is *idempotent* (so processing a duplicate has no additional effect), then the *effect* is exactly-once even though the *delivery* was not. You cannot get exactly-once *delivery*, but you can get exactly-once *effect*, and the way you get it is idempotent consumers. The mechanisms are the ones Chapter 18 and Chapter 17 supplied: the consumer records which message IDs it has already processed and ignores a repeat, or the operation is naturally idempotent by design, or it uses an idempotency key. This is not an optional refinement. In a distributed system using messaging — which is to say, in essentially every real distributed system — **idempotent consumers are a requirement**, because duplicates *will* happen, and a consumer that is not idempotent will, someday, process a duplicate and do the wrong thing twice. The idempotency mandate is the load-bearing pattern of distributed messaging, and a staff engineer treats "is this consumer idempotent?" as a non-negotiable question.

## 19.7 The Resilience Patterns Revisited

Chapter 17 introduced a set of resilience patterns in the single-service context. They are, fundamentally, *distributed-systems* patterns, and here they are seen as the deliberate, designed responses to the failure shapes of Section 19.3.

**Timeouts**, again and always. Every remote call has a finite timeout, because — Section 19.3 — the *slow node* is the worst failure, it does not announce itself, and a call with no timeout waiting on a slow node consumes resources indefinitely. The timeout is what *converts* the undetectable slow-node failure into a *detectable* one: with a timeout, a slow node produces a *timeout error*, which your code can see and respond to, instead of an infinite silent wait.

**Retries with backoff and jitter** — for transient failures, retry; with *exponential backoff* so retries do not hammer a struggling service; with *jitter* (randomization) so that many clients failing at once do not retry in synchronized waves. And — critically — retry only *idempotent* operations, because Section 19.6 just established that retries cause duplicates, and a non-idempotent operation retried is an operation done twice.

**The circuit breaker** — when a downstream is *hard down* (a crash, not a blip), stop calling it: the breaker opens, calls fail fast, the downstream gets room to recover. The circuit breaker is the designed response to the *crash* failure, the way the timeout is the designed response to the *slow node*.

**Bulkheads** — a pattern named for a ship's watertight compartments. The idea: *isolate* resources so that a failure in one part cannot consume the resources of the whole. If calls to downstream A and downstream B share one pool of workers, then A becoming slow can consume *every* worker and starve the calls to B — A's failure has flooded the whole ship. The bulkhead pattern gives A and B *separate* resource pools, so A's slowness fills only A's compartment and B keeps working. Bulkheads are how you stop one slow node from sinking everything.

**Backpressure, load shedding, graceful degradation** — as Chapter 17 defined them: resist being overwhelmed, shed excess load deliberately rather than collapsing, and let non-essential dependencies fail without failing the whole.

The unifying idea, and the chapter's design philosophy: in a distributed system, **failure is not an exceptional case to be handled as an afterthought — it is a constant, expected condition, and the system must be designed, from the start, to remain functional while parts of it are failing.** The resilience patterns are not add-ons. They are the structural response to the fact that, in a system of many machines, *something is essentially always failing somewhere*, and a design that only works when everything is healthy is a design that never actually works.

> ### War Story — The Retry Storm
>
> A company ran a system of services. One of them — call it the core service — was depended on by many others; a dozen different upstream services called it routinely. The core service was reliable, and the upstream services all, sensibly, *retried* failed calls to it, because retrying transient failures is good practice.
>
> But the retries were written naively. They retried *immediately* on failure, and they retried a *fixed* number of times at *fixed* intervals — no exponential backoff, and, crucially, *no jitter*.
>
> One day the core service had a brief problem — a few seconds of degradation, the kind of transient blip that retries are *supposed* to ride out gracefully. For those few seconds, calls to the core service failed. And because a dozen upstream services were all calling it, a dozen upstream services *all experienced the failure at the same moment* — and all, at the same moment, began retrying. With no jitter, they all retried at the *same fixed interval* — so a few seconds later, *all of them retried simultaneously*, in a synchronized wave. That synchronized wave of retries was itself a sudden, sharp *spike of load* on the core service — which was already not fully healthy. The retry spike pushed it from "briefly degraded" to "overloaded." Now *more* calls failed, so *more* retries were scheduled, all still synchronized, and the *next* wave was even larger. The retries, intended to ride out a transient blip, had instead formed a self-reinforcing feedback loop: failure caused synchronized retries, synchronized retries caused a load spike, the load spike caused more failure. The core service, which had had a *few-second* problem, was now *hard down* under a self-inflicted storm of its own clients' retries, and it stayed down — because every time it tried to recover, the next synchronized retry wave knocked it over again.
>
> Recovering required, in effect, *stopping the clients from retrying* long enough for the core service to stabilize. The permanent fix was three changes to every client's retry logic, each of them a pattern from this chapter. *Exponential backoff* — wait longer after each successive failure, so a struggling service is not hammered at a fixed fast rate. *Jitter* — randomize each client's backoff, so that a dozen clients failing at the same instant do *not* retry at the same instant; their retries spread out across time instead of arriving as a wave. And *circuit breakers* — so that once the core service was clearly down, the clients would stop calling it entirely for a cooling-off period, removing the retry load and giving it room to recover.
>
> The lesson is the chapter's. A retry is not a free, obviously-good thing — a *naive* retry, multiplied across many clients and synchronized by a shared failure, is an *amplifier*: it turns a small transient problem into a large sustained one. The network's failures interact, the clients' responses to failure interact, and a distributed system must be designed with that interaction in mind. Retries need backoff and jitter not as polish but as the difference between a retry that *dampens* a problem and a retry that *amplifies* it into an outage. The "retry storm" or "thundering herd" is a named, classic distributed-systems failure, and the patterns that prevent it — backoff, jitter, circuit breakers — are not optional refinements. They are the design.

## 19.8 Distributed Transactions and the Saga Pattern

Chapter 18's transactions — atomic, all-or-nothing — work beautifully *within one database*. But a distributed system's operation often spans *several* services, each with its *own* database: "place an order" might need the order service to record the order, the inventory service to reserve stock, and the payment service to take payment — three services, three databases, and the operation should be atomic: all three, or none.

The instinct is a *distributed transaction* — some mechanism that makes the three commits atomic across the three databases. Such mechanisms exist (the classic is "two-phase commit"), and the staff-level guidance is to *be very wary of them*: they are complex, they couple the services tightly, they tend to lock resources across the network for the duration, and they themselves fail in distributed ways. In modern distributed systems, true distributed transactions across services are generally *avoided*.

The pattern used instead is the **saga**. A saga reframes the multi-step distributed operation: instead of one atomic transaction, it is a *sequence of local transactions* — each service does its *own* local, atomic transaction — *plus*, for each step, a defined **compensating action** that *undoes* it. The saga executes the steps in order; if every step succeeds, the operation is complete. If a step *fails*, the saga does not roll back a distributed transaction (there isn't one) — it runs the *compensating actions* for all the steps that already *succeeded*, in reverse, *semantically undoing* them. The order is recorded; stock is reserved; payment *fails* — so the saga runs the compensations: un-reserve the stock, cancel the order. The system is returned to a consistent state not by an atomic rollback but by a sequence of explicit *undo* operations.

The saga is genuinely harder to think about than a transaction — you must design a *compensating action* for every step, and reason about the intermediate states the system passes through, and the saga is *not atomic* in the strict sense (there are moments when some steps are done and others not). But it is *workable* across services where a distributed transaction is not, and it is the standard pattern. Sagas can be *orchestrated* (a central coordinator drives the sequence) or *choreographed* (each service reacts to the previous step's event, often via the message broker of Section 19.5); both are used. And for complex, long-running sagas, there are *workflow engines* — systems specifically built to execute and track multi-step distributed workflows reliably, with the retries and compensations and state-tracking handled by the engine. The staff-level point: when an operation spans services, do not reach for a distributed transaction; reach for a saga — a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions — and accept the loss of strict atomicity as the price of a system that actually works across the network.

## 19.9 Observability in a Distributed System

A final theme, which Chapter 20 will make its whole subject, but which must be named here because it is *distributed systems* that make it essential. In a single program, when something goes wrong, you have a stack trace — a single, local, complete account of what happened. In a distributed system, a single user request might touch a dozen services across a dozen machines, and when it goes wrong, *there is no single stack trace* — the story of that request is *scattered* across a dozen machines' logs, and reconstructing it is, without the right tooling, nearly impossible.

This is why **observability** — the ability to understand what a system is doing from the outside — is not optional in a distributed system; it is a *requirement* for the system to be operable at all. The essential piece this chapter must name is **distributed tracing**: the practice of attaching a *trace identifier* to a request when it first enters the system, and *propagating* that identifier through *every* service the request touches, so that all the scattered log entries and timing data for that one request can be *stitched back together* into a single, coherent, end-to-end picture — this request entered here, called these services in this order, that one was slow, that one failed. Distributed tracing is what gives a distributed system back the thing a single program had for free: a complete account of what happened. Without it, debugging a distributed system is guesswork. Chapter 20 covers tracing, and logging, and metrics, and the whole discipline of observability properly; the point *here* is that the distributed nature of the system is precisely *why* that discipline is mandatory rather than nice-to-have.

> ### The Level Line — Distributed Systems
>
> **A senior engineer** can build a service that calls other services, knows the network can fail, and can use a message queue.
>
> **A staff engineer** holds the core truth that a distributed system is a different and harder kind of thing because of the network; knows the fallacies and the three shapes of failure, and that the slow node is the dangerous one; makes the CAP consistency/availability trade-off explicitly and per the data; chooses deliberately between synchronous and asynchronous communication; understands delivery semantics and treats the idempotency mandate as non-negotiable; applies the resilience patterns as the designed response to specific failure shapes; uses sagas rather than distributed transactions; and knows distributed tracing is mandatory.
>
> **A principal engineer** designs distributed systems where failure is assumed and survived by construction — where every remote interaction has its timeout and its circuit breaker, every consumer is idempotent, every multi-service operation is a saga, every piece of data has a consciously-chosen consistency model, and the whole is observable end-to-end; sets the organization's standards for distributed-systems design; and can teach the difference between a distributed system and a big program, which is the single most valuable thing one engineer can teach another about systems at scale.

## 19.10 At the Interview Table

This chapter is the core of the staff and principal *system-design interview*. The interviewer presents an open-ended system to design and watches, primarily, whether you reason like someone who knows the network is hostile.

**The question: "Design a [large-scale system]" (the system-design interview itself).**

There is a *structure* to a strong answer, and it is the structure this chapter and Chapters 12, 17, 18 have built. Clarify the requirements and the scale first. Sketch the high-level components and how they communicate — and *here* make the synchronous-versus-asynchronous choices explicitly. Address the data — the schema, and (Chapter 18) the storage choices, and (this chapter) the *consistency model* for each piece of data, made as a conscious CAP trade-off. Then — and this is where staff-level candidates separate themselves — *spend real time on failure*: "this service depends on that one, so the call has a timeout and a circuit breaker; this operation spans services, so it's a saga with these compensating actions; this consumer must be idempotent because delivery is at-least-once; this data chooses availability over consistency because brief staleness is acceptable here, but *this* data chooses consistency because it must be correct." Address scale, observability, deployment. The interviewer is not looking for the "right" architecture — there rarely is one — they are looking for whether you *reason about trade-offs*, whether you *assume failure*, and whether you can *justify* every choice. A candidate who designs only the happy path has failed the interview regardless of how elegant the happy path is.

**The question: "Why is exactly-once delivery hard? How do real systems handle duplicates?"**

"True exactly-once *delivery* is, in the general case, impossible over an unreliable network — the sender can never be certain a message arrived, because the acknowledgment itself can be lost, so it must either risk losing the message or risk sending it again; there's no way to be certain of exactly one. So real systems use *at-least-once* delivery — the sender retries until acknowledged, which never loses a message but *will* sometimes duplicate one when an ack is lost. And then they make the *consumer idempotent* — processing a duplicate has no additional effect, by recording processed message IDs, or designing the operation to be naturally idempotent, or using an idempotency key. You can't get exactly-once delivery, but at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer gives you exactly-once *effect*. In any distributed system using messaging, idempotent consumers are a hard requirement, because duplicates will happen."

**The question: "Explain the CAP theorem."**

"It's about three properties of a distributed system: consistency — every read sees the latest write — availability — every request gets a response — and partition tolerance — the system keeps working despite the network splitting. The theorem says that when a partition happens — and in any real system partitions *will* happen, so partition tolerance isn't optional — you must choose between consistency and availability. During a partition the system is split into halves that can't communicate; a write arrives at one half, and either the system refuses it to stay consistent (sacrificing availability) or accepts it and lets the halves diverge (sacrificing consistency). There's no third option. So it's a design decision, made per the data: for a bank balance or an inventory count, you choose consistency, because stale or conflicting data is a correctness failure; for a social feed or a view count, you choose availability, because brief inconsistency is fine. The theorem doesn't tell you which — it tells you that you *must* choose, and that the choice depends on what the data is."

**The question: "How do you handle a transaction that spans multiple services?"**

"Not with a distributed transaction — two-phase commit and the like are complex, tightly coupling, and fragile, and modern distributed systems avoid them. The pattern is a *saga*: instead of one atomic transaction across services, a *sequence of local transactions* — each service does its own atomic local transaction — plus a *compensating action* for each step that semantically undoes it. The saga runs the steps in order; if one fails, it runs the compensating actions for the steps that already succeeded, in reverse, to return the system to consistency. It's not atomic in the strict sense — there are intermediate states — and it's harder to design, since you need an undo for every step, but it works across services where a distributed transaction doesn't. Sagas can be orchestrated by a coordinator or choreographed through events, and for complex ones there are workflow engines built to run them reliably."

**The question: "Tell me about retries." (or, after a War-Story-style prompt)**

"Retries handle transient failures, but naive retries are dangerous — they can *amplify* a small problem into an outage. Three things make a retry safe. Exponential backoff — wait longer after each failure, so you don't hammer a struggling service. Jitter — randomize the backoff, because if many clients fail at the same moment and retry at the same fixed interval, their retries arrive as a synchronized wave, and that wave is itself a load spike that can knock over a service that was only briefly degraded — the retry storm. And retry only *idempotent* operations, because a retry can cause a duplicate, and retrying a non-idempotent operation does it twice. For a downstream that's hard down rather than blipping, add a circuit breaker so retries stop entirely and the downstream gets room to recover."

**The red flags.** Designing only the happy path in a system-design interview — never mentioning failure, timeouts, partitions, consistency trade-offs. Treating a remote call like a local call. Believing exactly-once delivery is achievable, or not knowing the idempotency mandate. Not knowing CAP, or stating it as a rule rather than a per-data trade-off. Proposing a distributed transaction across services. Naive retries with no backoff or jitter. And, the most fundamental, talking about a distributed system as though it were just a large program — the single clearest sign of an engineer who has not yet made the conceptual leap this chapter exists to deliver.

## 19.11 The Forge

**Drill 19.1.** For each scenario, name which of the three failure shapes it is and what makes it easy or hard to handle: a downstream service's process has crashed; a downstream service is up but a network link to it is severed; a downstream service is responding but taking ten times longer than usual.

**Drill 19.2.** For each piece of data, state whether it needs strong consistency or can tolerate eventual consistency, and justify via the CAP trade-off: a bank account balance; the number of likes on a post; a product's inventory count; a user's display name; a "currently online" indicator.

**Build 19.1.** Build a small two-service system that communicates through a message broker (or a simple stand-in). Make the delivery at-least-once by having the sender retry, and *deliberately* cause duplicate delivery. Then make the consumer idempotent — recording processed message IDs — and demonstrate that a duplicated message produces exactly-once *effect*. Write up the idempotency mandate as you experienced it.

**Build 19.2.** Implement a saga: a multi-step operation across three simulated services, each step a local transaction, each with a compensating action. Demonstrate the success path, and then force a failure at the third step and demonstrate the compensating actions undoing the first two. Write up the intermediate states the system passed through.

**Build 19.3.** Implement and demonstrate a retry storm, then prevent it. Build a service called by several simulated clients with *naive* retries (immediate, fixed interval, no jitter); cause a brief failure and observe the synchronized retry waves amplifying it. Then add exponential backoff, jitter, and a circuit breaker, and show the same brief failure now being absorbed gracefully.

**Investigate 19.1.** Take a system you know (or design one) and audit it against the fallacies of distributed computing: for each fallacy, find a place where the system assumes it, and assess the consequence. Write up what you found.

**Investigate 19.2.** Implement basic distributed tracing across the two-service system from Build 19.1: generate a trace ID when a request enters, propagate it through both services, and include it in every log line. Then reconstruct the full story of one request from the combined logs. Write up why this is impossible without the propagated ID.

**Design 19.1.** You are the staff engineer designing a distributed system of meaningful scale — pick a concrete one (a ride-hailing backend, an e-commerce order system, a payment platform). Write a four-to-five-page design document: the services and their responsibilities; for each inter-service interaction, synchronous or asynchronous and why; for each piece of data, its consistency model as a conscious CAP choice; the resilience patterns and which failure shape each addresses; the multi-service operations as sagas with their compensating actions; the idempotency strategy for every consumer; how the system is observable end-to-end; and — running through all of it — an explicit account of how the system behaves when each part fails. This is the system-design interview, full-length, on paper. It is the capstone of the technical half of the book.

# Part V Capstone — A Resilient, Distributed, Observable Service

Part V has been the leap from programs to systems — services, data, and the distributed world. The capstone is, deliberately, the largest in the book so far, because it is the shape of real staff-level system work: design and build a small but genuinely distributed system that is correct, resilient, and observable, and that survives the failures the network will throw at it.

**The brief.** Build a system of at least three communicating services around a real domain — for example, an order-processing system with an order service, an inventory service, and a payment/notification service. The services must communicate (some synchronously, some via a message broker), must persist state, must coordinate a multi-service operation, and must remain functional when parts of them fail.

**What it must demonstrate, by chapter:**

From **Chapter 17** — each service is a hardened web service: every external call has a timeout, connections are pooled, downstream calls have retries-with-backoff-and-jitter and circuit breakers, the services degrade gracefully and shut down gracefully.

From **Chapter 18** — each service persists its state correctly: parameterized queries throughout, an ORM used with awareness of the N+1 problem, transactions where operations are composite, a deliberate consistency choice for each piece of data, the data layer decoupled behind repositories, and a thought-through migration discipline.

From **Chapter 19** — the system is genuinely distributed and genuinely resilient: the synchronous-versus-asynchronous communication choices made deliberately; the consistency model for each piece of data chosen as a conscious CAP trade-off; the multi-service "place an order" operation implemented as a *saga* with compensating actions; every message consumer made *idempotent* under at-least-once delivery; the resilience patterns applied as designed responses to specific failure shapes; and the whole system instrumented with distributed tracing so a request can be followed end-to-end.

It must also carry forward the whole book: it is *concurrent* where appropriate (Part IV), *architected* with layering and ports and dependency injection (Part III), *typed* and *tested* — including tests of the failure paths and the saga's compensations — and *packaged* with the modern stack.

**The deliverables:**

1. The distributed system itself — at least three services, communicating, persisting, coordinating, resilient.
2. A test suite that exercises the happy paths *and the failure paths* — a downstream timing out, a service crashing mid-saga, a duplicated message — proving the system survives them.
3. A demonstration of distributed tracing: a single request followed end-to-end across all the services from the combined, trace-correlated logs.
4. A *system-design document* — five to seven pages — that is, in effect, the written form of a staff-level system-design interview: the architecture, every synchronous/asynchronous and consistency and resilience decision with its justification, the saga design, the idempotency strategy, the failure analysis (how the system behaves when each part fails), and the trade-offs accepted.

**On the interview.** This capstone *is* the system-design interview, built rather than merely discussed. The staff and principal system-design rounds ask you to design exactly this kind of system, out loud, in forty-five minutes — and the engineer who has actually *built* one, who has watched their own saga compensate a failure and their own circuit breaker open and their own idempotent consumer swallow a duplicate, will reason about that interview from experience rather than from theory. Build it as the definitive rehearsal of the technical half of this book — and then turn to Part VI, which is everything that happens *after* a system like this is built: running it, watching it, securing it, and surviving it in production.
