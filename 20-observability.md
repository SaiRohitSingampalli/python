## 20.1 The Question Behind the Chapter

Here is a situation every engineer who has operated a real system has lived through. The system is misbehaving — slow, or erroring, or simply *wrong* — and you are staring at it, and you *cannot tell why*. The code is all there; you can read every line. But reading the code tells you what the system *can* do, not what it *is* doing, right now, in production, under real load, with real data. The gap between those two — between the code you can read and the behavior you cannot see — is the gap that observability exists to close.

**Observability is the ability to understand what a system is doing, and why, from the outside** — from the signals it emits, without attaching a debugger, without reproducing the problem locally, without guessing. And it is not a luxury. A system you cannot observe is a system you cannot *operate*: you cannot tell if it is healthy, you cannot find out why it is not, you cannot know whether a deploy made things better or worse, you are flying blind. For a distributed system (Chapter 19) it is worse than blind — there is no single stack trace, the story of a failure is scattered across a dozen machines, and without observability you cannot even *assemble* the story, let alone understand it.

This chapter is about the discipline and the tools of seeing. The "three pillars" — logs, metrics, traces — and what each is for. The principle that observability must be *designed in*, not bolted on after an outage. The signals that actually matter, so that you measure what tells you the system is healthy rather than what is merely easy to measure. And the connection to *alerting* — turning observation into a timely "wake up, something is wrong" — which is the bridge to Chapter 23. A staff engineer treats observability as a first-class part of building a system, exactly as much as the features, because a feature you cannot see in production is a feature you cannot truly be said to have shipped.

## 20.2 The Three Pillars

Observability is conventionally built on three kinds of signal — the "three pillars" — and they are genuinely different things, each answering a different question. A staff engineer knows what each is *for*, because using the wrong pillar for a question is a common and wasteful mistake.

**Logs** are *discrete, timestamped records of events* — "a request arrived for this path," "this operation failed with this error," "this user logged in." A log is a *narrative*: read in sequence, logs tell the story of what happened. Logs answer the question **"what, exactly, happened?"** — they are where you go for the *detail* of a specific event or a specific request. Their strength is richness and specificity; their weakness is volume and cost — you cannot log everything about everything, and a system that does drowns the signal in noise and the budget in storage.

**Metrics** are *aggregated numerical measurements over time* — "requests per second," "error rate," "p99 latency," "memory in use," "queue depth." A metric is not a narrative; it is a *number, sampled repeatedly*, producing a time series. Metrics answer the question **"what is the overall state and trend?"** — they are where you go to know whether the *system as a whole* is healthy, whether something is getting worse, whether a deploy moved a number. Their strength is that they are cheap to store and fast to query even over long spans, so they are what you *graph*, what you *alert on*, what you *watch*. Their weakness is the flip side: a metric tells you the error rate *rose*; it does not tell you *which* errors or *why* — for that you need the logs.

**Traces** are the distributed-systems pillar of Chapter 19. A trace follows a *single request* through *every* service and operation it touches, stitching the scattered pieces into one end-to-end picture by means of a propagated trace ID. A trace answers the question **"for this one request, where did the time go and where did it fail?"** — it is where you go to understand the *path* of a request across a distributed system, to see that the request was slow *because* service C took 800 milliseconds, to see *which* hop in a dozen-service journey broke.

The three are *complementary*, and the skilled operator moves between them. A *metric* alerts you that the error rate has risen — the metric noticed. You look at *traces* to see *which* requests are failing and *where* in their journey — the trace localizes. You look at the *logs* for those specific failing requests to see the *exact* error and detail — the log explains. Metric to trace to log: notice, localize, explain. An engineer who only has logs is overwhelmed and slow; one who only has metrics knows something is wrong but not what; one who has all three, and knows which to reach for, can go from "alert fired" to "root cause" in minutes. That is what observability buys, and it is why the chapter treats all three as essential rather than picking a favorite.

## 20.3 Logging Done Well

Logging is the pillar every engineer already uses, and uses badly. Doing it *well* has a few principles that separate a log stream that helps from one that hurts.

**Structured logging.** The single most important upgrade. An *unstructured* log line is a string — `"User 4521 failed to update order 9912: timeout"` — readable by a human, but *opaque to a machine*: to find "all failures for order 9912" you must parse that string with fragile pattern-matching. A *structured* log line is a record of *key-value fields* — an event name, and `user_id=4521`, `order_id=9912`, `error=timeout`, `duration_ms=5031` — typically emitted as JSON. The difference is transformative: structured logs can be *queried* like data — "show me every failed event for `order_id=9912`," "count events where `error=timeout` grouped by `service`" — because the fields are *fields*, not substrings. In any system of meaningful size, **logs should be structured**, full stop; the libraries make it easy (`structlog` is the common Python choice, and the standard library's `logging` can be configured to emit JSON). A staff engineer building a new service sets up structured logging from the first commit, because retrofitting it later means rewriting every log call.

**Log levels, used with discipline.** Logging frameworks have *levels* — `DEBUG`, `INFO`, `WARNING`, `ERROR`, `CRITICAL` — and they are a tool for *separating signal from noise*, but only if used with discipline. The discipline: a level means a specific thing. `ERROR` means *something failed that needs attention* — and therefore `ERROR` should be *rare* and every `ERROR` should be *real*, because if `ERROR` is used for things that are routine, the level becomes noise and the genuine errors hide in it. `WARNING` means *something unusual but handled*. `INFO` is the normal narrative of operation. `DEBUG` is detail for diagnosis, normally turned off in production. The failure mode is *level inflation* — logging routine things at `ERROR`, logging so much at `INFO` that nothing can be found — and the cure is discipline: each level means what it means, and `ERROR` in particular is *sacred*, reserved for things that genuinely warrant a human's attention.

**Correlation.** A log line in isolation is of limited use; a log line you can connect to *the request it belongs to* and to *every other log line from that same request* is far more powerful. This is the *correlation ID* (or request ID, or — in a distributed system — the trace ID of Section 20.5): an identifier generated when a request enters the system and *attached to every log line* emitted while handling it. With it, "show me everything that happened for this one request" is a single query. Without it, the log lines of a thousand concurrent requests are interleaved into an unreadable mess. Correlation is what makes a structured log stream *navigable*.

**What not to log — and this one is a hard rule.** Logs **must never contain secrets or sensitive personal data** — no passwords, no API keys, no tokens, no credit-card numbers, no more personal data than is genuinely needed. The reason is severe: logs are *widely accessible* — many engineers can read them, they are shipped to log-aggregation systems, they are retained — so a secret in a log is a secret *leaked* to everyone with log access and everywhere the logs are stored, and a logged credential is a real, exploitable security incident. It is dismayingly easy to do by accident — logging a whole request object that *contains* an auth header, logging an exception whose message *includes* a sensitive value. A staff engineer treats "no secrets, no unnecessary personal data, in logs, ever" as an absolute rule, sets up *redaction* (automatic stripping of sensitive fields) as a safety net, and watches for this specifically in code review. (Chapter 22 returns to this; it is named here because logging is where the leak most often happens.)

## 20.4 Metrics and the Signals That Matter

Metrics are the pillar you *watch* and *alert on*, and the central skill is not collecting metrics — collecting them is easy — but knowing *which* metrics matter, because it is entirely possible to collect a thousand metrics and still not know whether the system is healthy.

There are two well-known frameworks for "which metrics matter," and they agree more than they differ. The **Four Golden Signals** (from Google's site-reliability practice) say to measure, for any service: **Latency** — how long requests take (and, recall Chapter 15, measured in *percentiles*, p99 and p99.9, not averages, because the tail is where the suffering is); **Traffic** — how much demand, requests per second; **Errors** — the rate of failing requests; and **Saturation** — how *full* the system's resources are, how close to a limit (CPU, memory, queue depth, connection-pool usage). The related **RED method** — **Rate, Errors, Duration** — is a simpler subset focused on request-handling services. Either way, the lesson is the same: a small number of *carefully chosen* signals tells you whether a service is healthy, and a staff engineer instruments those *first* and deliberately, rather than collecting metrics at random and hoping the important one is among them.

Two distinctions a staff engineer should hold. First, the difference between **system metrics** and **business metrics**. System metrics (CPU, memory, latency, error rate) tell you whether the *infrastructure* is healthy. *Business* metrics — orders placed per minute, sign-ups, payments processed, items shipped — tell you whether the system is doing its *actual job*. Both matter, and the second is sometimes the better alarm: a subtle bug can leave every system metric perfectly green while *orders placed* quietly drops to zero — the infrastructure is "healthy" and the business is *broken*. The most informative dashboards pair the two, and the most reliable alerts often watch a *business* metric, because the business metric is the one that, by definition, tracks whether the system is delivering value.

Second — and this is genuine staff-level judgment — beware **measuring what is easy instead of what matters**. It is easy to measure CPU usage; it is harder to measure "the p99 latency of the checkout flow as the customer experiences it." The easy metric is tempting and often nearly useless; the metric that *matters* is the one tied to the user's actual experience or the business's actual outcome, and it usually takes more thought to instrument. A staff engineer resists the easy-metric trap and asks, of every dashboard, "does this actually tell us the system is doing its job?"

## 20.5 Tracing and the Distributed Picture

Tracing is the pillar Chapter 19 named as mandatory for distributed systems, and this section gives it its proper treatment.

The problem tracing solves: in a distributed system, a single user request fans out across many services, and when that request is slow or fails, the *cause* is somewhere in that fan-out — but the information about the request is *scattered* across all the machines it touched, with nothing connecting the pieces. You can see service A's logs and service C's logs, but you cannot tell that *this* slow request in A's logs is the *same request* as *that* error in C's logs.

A **distributed trace** solves it with a propagated identifier. When a request enters the system, it is assigned a **trace ID**. That trace ID is then *propagated* — passed along — to *every* downstream service the request triggers, typically in the request headers, so every service handling any part of the request *knows the trace ID*. Within the trace, each individual operation — each service's handling, each database query, each downstream call — is recorded as a **span**, with its own start and end time and its place in the hierarchy (this span happened *within* that one). The collection of all the spans sharing a trace ID, assembled, *is* the end-to-end picture: a timeline of the entire request across the entire system, showing exactly which operation ran when, which was slow, which failed, and how they nested.

What this *gives* you is the thing a single program had for free and a distributed system otherwise lacks: a complete, coherent account of one request. A trace view shows, at a glance, "this checkout request took 1.4 seconds; 1.1 of those seconds were the inventory service; within that, a single slow database query." Without tracing, finding that would be hours of cross-referencing logs across machines; with tracing, it is one view.

The standard a staff engineer should know by name is **OpenTelemetry** — a vendor-neutral, industry-wide standard for generating and collecting traces (and metrics and logs). Its importance is the *neutrality*: you instrument your code once, against the OpenTelemetry standard, and you can send the resulting telemetry to *any* compatible backend, and switch backends without re-instrumenting. For new systems, instrumenting with OpenTelemetry is the modern, sound default, and it is the thing that makes the distributed picture of Chapter 19 actually attainable.

## 20.6 Observability Is Designed In, Not Bolted On

A principle that runs underneath the whole chapter and deserves to be stated directly: **observability is a property you design into a system from the beginning, not a thing you add after an outage.**

The anti-pattern is universal and tempting. A team builds a system, focused entirely on features; it works; it ships. Then, in production, it misbehaves — and the team discovers, in the middle of the incident, that they *cannot see why*, because there are no useful metrics, the logs are unstructured and uncorrelated, there is no tracing. So *during the outage*, under pressure, they scramble to add logging and metrics, deploy the instrumentation, wait for the problem to recur, and only then begin to actually diagnose. The instrumentation that should have been there from the start is being built, badly, at the worst possible time.

The reason observability must be designed in is partly practical — retrofitting structured logging and tracing means touching a great deal of code — but mostly it is about *when you need it*. You need observability *during* an incident, and an incident is exactly when you cannot afford to be *building* it. The instrumentation must already be there, already emitting, already on the dashboards, *before* the problem, because the problem will not wait for you to get ready. So a staff engineer treats observability as part of the *definition of done*: a service is not "finished" when the feature works; it is finished when the feature works *and* it emits structured logs, *and* it reports the golden-signal metrics, *and* it participates in tracing, *and* there are dashboards, *and* there are alerts. "Can we see this in production?" is a question asked at *design* time, in code review, as a first-class requirement — not a question discovered, painfully, at 3 a.m.

There is a related, deeper idea worth naming: **build for debuggability**. Beyond the three pillars, a system should be *designed* so that its failures are *diagnosable* — clear and specific error messages (Chapter 8), errors that carry context, exceptions chained to preserve the cause, log events rich enough to reconstruct what happened, IDs that correlate. A system can be made easier or harder to debug *by how it is built*, and a staff engineer builds for the future debugger — who will often be themselves, at 3 a.m., grateful or cursing depending on choices made calmly months before.

## 20.7 From Observation to Alerting

Observability is *seeing*; **alerting** is being *told* — automatically, promptly — that something needs attention, so that a human does not have to be staring at a dashboard to notice. Alerting is the bridge from this chapter to Chapter 23 (incidents and on-call), and a few principles belong here, where the signals are defined.

The first and most important principle: **alert on symptoms, not causes — and specifically, alert on things that affect users.** The instinct is to alert on causes — "CPU is high," "a server is down" — but a cause-based alert is often a false alarm (high CPU that no user notices is not a problem worth waking someone for) and often misses real problems (the users are suffering and the CPU is fine). A *symptom*-based alert watches the thing that *actually matters* — "the error rate users are experiencing has risen," "p99 latency has crossed the threshold," "orders-placed-per-minute has dropped." A symptom alert fires when, and only when, *users are actually affected*, which is exactly when a human should be woken. The cause — which server, which CPU — is then found by *investigation*, using the observability tools; the alert's job is to reliably catch *real user impact*, not to guess at causes.

The second principle, just as important: **every alert must be actionable, and alert fatigue is a real and dangerous failure.** An alert that fires and requires no action — an alert a human looks at and says "yeah, that's normal, ignore it" — is *worse than no alert*, because it teaches the on-call engineer to *ignore alerts*. And once alerts are routinely ignored, the *real* alert — the one that matters — is also ignored, lost in the noise, indistinguishable from the dozen false ones. This is exactly the dynamic of Chapter 16's flaky test, in a new domain: noise destroys trust, and a distrusted signal is a useless signal. So the discipline is ruthless: every alert must correspond to a real problem requiring real human action *now*; an alert that does not is *tuned* until it does, or *deleted*. A small number of trustworthy, actionable alerts is vastly better than a large number of noisy ones — and a staff engineer treats alert *quality* as something to be actively maintained, pruning the noise so the signal survives.

> ### War Story — The Outage Nobody Could See
>
> A team operated a service that had been built, capably, by engineers focused on getting the features right. It worked. It had been in production for months. It had logging — the ordinary kind, unstructured string messages, written wherever an engineer had thought to write one — and it had the basic infrastructure metrics that came for free from the hosting platform: CPU, memory, instance count. No structured logging, no correlation IDs, no business metrics, no tracing, and a handful of alerts on CPU and memory.
>
> One day, the service started failing — not completely, but partially: a meaningful fraction of a particular kind of user request began returning errors. No infrastructure alert fired, because CPU and memory were fine; the failure was in the *logic*, not the *resources*. The first the team learned of it was *customer complaints*, hours after it began — hours during which the system was visibly broken to users and invisibly broken to the team.
>
> Then the diagnosis began, and the diagnosis was the real lesson. The team had no metric for the error rate of that request type, so they could not even *see the shape* of the problem — how many requests, which ones, since when — they had only the anecdotes from complaints. They went to the logs, and the logs were a flood of unstructured strings from thousands of concurrent requests, interleaved, with *no correlation IDs*, so they could not isolate the log lines belonging to a single *failing* request from the thousands of lines belonging to successful ones. There was no tracing, so when they suspected a downstream dependency, they could not confirm it — could not see a request's path. So they did the thing the previous section named as the anti-pattern: *during the outage*, under pressure, with customers affected, they began *adding* observability — writing structured log statements, adding an error-rate metric, adding correlation — deploying that instrumentation, and then *waiting for the problem to recur* so they could finally watch it happen. The incident lasted far longer than the underlying bug warranted, and almost all of that length was not fixing the bug — it was *building, mid-incident, the ability to see the bug*.
>
> When the instrumentation was finally in place, the actual cause was found and fixed quickly — it was a modest bug, the kind of thing that, in an observable system, would have been a fifteen-minute investigation. The team's retrospective conclusion was unanimous and is this chapter's thesis: the outage had not really been long because the bug was hard. It had been long because the *system could not be seen*, and a system that cannot be seen cannot be diagnosed, and you cannot build the ability to see it *after* you need it. They made observability part of the definition of done — structured logs, correlation IDs, golden-signal and business metrics, tracing, dashboards, and symptom-based alerts, all required *before* a service shipped — and the next incident of comparable underlying difficulty was resolved in a fraction of the time, because the instrumentation that mattered was already there, already running, already on a screen, *before* anyone needed it.

> ### The Level Line — Observability
>
> **A senior engineer** writes logs, knows metrics and dashboards exist, can read a dashboard, and can investigate a problem when given the tools.
>
> **A staff engineer** treats observability as a designed-in, first-class part of every system; knows the three pillars and which to reach for; sets up structured logging with correlation IDs and disciplined levels and no secrets; instruments the golden signals and the business metrics, resisting the easy-metric trap; ensures distributed tracing exists; and builds alerting that is symptom-based, user-focused, and ruthlessly free of noise.
>
> **A principal engineer** makes observability a standard and a culture — part of the organization's definition of done, with the instrumentation, the dashboards, and the alerting expected before a system ships; ensures systems are built for debuggability; treats the quality of the signal — actionable alerts, navigable logs, meaningful metrics — as something actively owned and maintained; and teaches that a system you cannot see is a system you cannot operate.

## 20.8 At the Interview Table

Observability questions appear in system-design rounds and in operational and behavioral rounds, and they reliably separate engineers who have *operated* systems from those who have only *built* them.

**The question: "How would you make this system observable?" (often a closing part of a system-design interview)**

"I'd build in the three pillars from the start. *Structured logging* — JSON, key-value fields, queryable — with a correlation ID on every request so all the log lines for one request can be pulled together, disciplined log levels so `ERROR` stays meaningful, and absolutely no secrets in the logs. *Metrics* — the four golden signals for every service: latency in percentiles, traffic, error rate, saturation — plus *business* metrics like orders-per-minute, because a bug can leave every system metric green while the business metric quietly flatlines. *Distributed tracing* — a trace ID propagated through every service, ideally via OpenTelemetry, so I can follow a single request end-to-end and see which hop was slow or failed. Then dashboards, and *alerting on symptoms* — user-facing things like error rate and p99 latency — not on causes like CPU. And critically, all of this is part of the definition of done, designed in, not added after the first outage, because observability is exactly what you need *during* an incident and an incident is the worst time to be building it."

**The question: "Logs, metrics, traces — what's the difference and when do you use each?"**

"They answer different questions. Logs are discrete timestamped event records — a narrative — and they answer 'what exactly happened?'; they're where I go for the detail of a specific event, rich but high-volume. Metrics are aggregated numbers over time — error rate, p99 latency, requests per second — and they answer 'what's the overall state and trend?'; they're cheap to store and fast to query, so they're what I graph and alert on, but they tell me *that* something's wrong, not *what*. Traces follow one request across all the services it touches via a propagated trace ID, and they answer 'for this request, where did the time go and where did it fail?' The skill is moving between them: a metric alerts me the error rate rose, traces show me which requests and where they broke, logs show me the exact error. Notice, localize, explain."

**The question: "What makes a good alert?"**

"It's symptom-based and actionable. Symptom-based means it watches what affects users — error rate, p99 latency, a dropping business metric — not causes like high CPU, because a cause-based alert both false-alarms and misses real problems; a symptom alert fires when and only when users are actually affected, which is when a human should be woken. Actionable means every alert corresponds to a real problem needing real action *now*. An alert that fires and needs no action is worse than no alert, because it trains the on-call engineer to ignore alerts — and then the real one gets ignored too, lost in the noise. So I'd keep a small number of trustworthy alerts and ruthlessly tune or delete anything that fires without being actionable. Alert fatigue is a real failure mode and alert quality has to be actively maintained."

**The question: "How would you debug a slow endpoint in production?"**

"I'd use the observability stack rather than guessing. Start with metrics — confirm it's actually slow, see the p99, see *when* it started, correlate that with a deploy. Then traces — pull traces for the slow requests and see *where* the time goes; a trace usually points straight at the slow hop, a particular downstream or a particular query. Then logs for those specific slow requests, found by correlation ID, for the exact detail. If the system *weren't* observable, my honest answer is that I'd be much slower and partly guessing — which is exactly why observability has to be built in before it's needed."

**The red flags.** Treating observability as something added after launch. Knowing only logs, with no metrics or tracing. Logging secrets, or no awareness that it is a hazard. Alerting on causes, or no concept of alert fatigue. Measuring only easy infrastructure metrics with no business metrics. And, generally, an engineer who talks fluently about *building* a system but cannot say how they would *see* it running — a clear sign of building experience without operating experience.

## 20.9 The Forge

**Drill 20.1.** Take a service with unstructured string logging and convert it to structured logging — key-value fields, JSON output, a correlation ID attached to every log line for a request. Then write a query (or pseudo-query) that would have been impossible before and is trivial now, such as "all failed events for one specific order."

**Drill 20.2.** For a service of your choice, define the four golden signals concretely — exactly what you would measure for latency (which percentiles), traffic, errors, and saturation — and then define two *business* metrics for the same service. Explain what a bug would look like that leaves all four golden signals green while a business metric drops.

**Build 20.1.** Instrument a small service with all three pillars: structured logging with correlation IDs, golden-signal metrics, and distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry, with a trace ID propagated if the service calls a downstream). Stand up a dashboard. Then introduce a fault and practice diagnosing it by moving metric → trace → log.

**Build 20.2.** Build an alerting setup for a service: define a small set of symptom-based, user-focused alerts with thresholds, and deliberately also write one *bad* alert (cause-based or non-actionable). Trigger conditions for each. Then write up why the bad alert is bad and how alert fatigue would set in if a system had many like it.

**Investigate 20.1.** Take a real system (yours or open-source) and audit its observability honestly: are the logs structured and correlated? Are the golden signals measured? Is there tracing? Are there business metrics? Are the alerts symptom-based and actionable? Write up the gaps and what you would add, in priority order.

**Investigate 20.2.** Find a piece of code that logs something sensitive — a whole request object, an exception that includes a secret, a user record with personal data. (If you cannot find one, write one.) Then implement redaction that strips sensitive fields automatically, and write up how a secret ends up in logs by accident and why that is a real security incident.

**Design 20.1.** You are the staff engineer responsible for the observability of a distributed system of several services. Write a two-to-three-page observability design: the logging standard (structure, correlation, levels, redaction); the metrics every service must emit (golden signals plus business metrics) and the dashboards; the tracing approach; the alerting philosophy and an initial set of alerts; and — explicitly — how observability is made part of the definition of done so it is designed in rather than bolted on. This is the staff-level exercise: observability as a designed, owned, organization-wide property, not an afterthought.
