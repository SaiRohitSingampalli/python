## 28.1 The Question Behind the Chapter

Chapter 26 established the defining shift of staff-level work: impact achieved *through other people*. This chapter is about the two most direct, most everyday mechanisms of that shift — **code review** and **mentorship** — the two activities through which an engineer most concretely *multiplies* the engineers around them.

The framing the chapter is built on is a piece of arithmetic worth making explicit. An engineer working alone produces, at most, one engineer's worth of output. An engineer who makes *ten other engineers each ten* *percent better* has produced, in effect, *one additional engineer's worth of output* — and has done so in a way that *compounds*, because those ten engineers carry their improvement forward into everything they subsequently do, and pass it on in turn. This is **leverage**, and it is the central concept of staff-level effectiveness: the highest-impact engineers are not, past a certain point, the ones who *do the most themselves* — they are the ones who *raise the people around them*, because raising people scales and doing-it-yourself does not. Code review and mentorship are the two most direct levers of that multiplication, performed not occasionally but *constantly*, woven into the normal week — and so doing them *well* is not a peripheral nicety but a core, high-leverage part of a staff engineer's actual job.

This chapter teaches both. Code review — what it is genuinely for (which is more than catching bugs), how to do it so it *teaches* and *builds* rather than *discourages*, and the disciplines of both giving and receiving it. And mentorship — the difference between mentoring and sponsoring, how to actually develop another engineer, and the deeper principle that a staff engineer's job includes *making more staff engineers*.

## 28.2 What Code Review Is Really For

Most engineers think code review exists to *catch bugs*. It does catch bugs, and that is valuable — but if catching bugs were its only purpose, code review would be a poor use of the very expensive engineering time it consumes, because automated tools (the linters, type checkers, and tests of Parts III and IV) catch many classes of bug more reliably and far more cheaply than a human reviewer does. The reason code review is worth its real cost is that it does *several* things, and a staff engineer who understands all of them reviews very differently from one who thinks they are only bug-hunting.

Code review **catches bugs and design problems** — yes — and notably the *kind* a human catches that a tool cannot: not the syntax error or the type mismatch, but the *design* flaw, the missing edge case, the subtle logic error, the approach that will not scale, the security hole. A reviewer brings *judgment*, and judgment is exactly what the automated tools lack.

Code review **spreads knowledge.** When an engineer reviews a change, they *learn the part of the system the* *change touches* — so review steadily distributes understanding of the codebase across the team, and is one of the strongest defenses against the "only one person understands this" risk that makes a team fragile. Code review **teaches.** A review is a moment of *direct, specific, contextual feedback* on real work — and that is one of the most powerful teaching opportunities that exists. A thoughtful review comment does not just fix this change; it makes the author *better*, permanently, at the thing the comment was about. Review is, done well, a continuous mentorship channel running through the whole team's normal work.

Code review **maintains consistency and shared standards.** It is how a team's conventions, patterns, and quality bar are actually *upheld* in practice — not as a document, but as a living norm enforced gently, change by change.

And code review **creates shared ownership and accountability.** A change that two people have looked at is a change *the team* owns, not just its author — and the knowledge that one's work will be read by a respected colleague is, quietly, a real and healthy pressure toward care.

So the staff-level reframing: code review is not a *gate* that bugs must get past. It is a *teaching,* *knowledge-spreading, standard-maintaining, team-building* practice that *also* catches bugs. An engineer who reviews with all of those purposes in mind — who asks not only "is this correct?" but "what can the author learn here? what does the team need to know about this? is this consistent with how we do things?" — extracts far more value from review, and gives far more, than one who is only hunting for defects.

## 28.3 How to Give a Good Review

Giving code review well is a genuine skill, and it has both a *technical* dimension (what to look for) and — more difficult and more important — a *human* dimension (how to deliver it so it builds the author up rather than tearing them down). A staff engineer must be good at both.

**What to look for, in rough priority order.** Review attention is finite, so spend it on what matters most. *Correctness* — does the code do what it is supposed to, and handle the edge cases and the failure paths (Chapter 8)? *Design* — is the approach sound; does it fit the architecture; will it age well; is this the right *shape* of solution (Chapter 12)? *Clarity and maintainability* — will the next engineer to read this understand it; is it as simple as it can be? *Tests* — is the change adequately tested, and are the tests good ones (Chapter 16)? *Security* — does it introduce any of the vulnerabilities of Chapter 22? And — explicitly *lower* priority — *style and* *minor preference*. The priority order matters because a common failure of reviewing is *inversion* of it: a review full of nitpicks about naming and formatting while a genuine design flaw sails through is a review that spent its attention exactly wrong. (And much of the *low* end of that list — style, formatting — should be handled by *automated tooling*, not human comments, freeing the human reviewer to spend their judgment on the things that need judgment. This is Chapter 11's `ruff` paying off as review hygiene: let the tool argue about formatting so the people can discuss design.)

**How to deliver it — the human dimension, which matters more.** A review comment lands on a *person*, who put effort and some ego into the work, and how the comment is delivered determines whether it *teaches* or *wounds*, whether it builds the author's skill and confidence or erodes them. The principles:

*Be kind, and assume good intent.* The author did their honest best with what they knew; a review written from that assumption — collaborative, not adversarial — is received as help. A review written as though the author were careless or foolish is received as an attack, and an attacked author learns defensiveness, not engineering.

*Critique the code, never the person.* "This function has a problem" — not "you don't understand this." The work is the subject; the person is a colleague.

*Explain the why.* A comment that says only "change this" *corrects*; a comment that says "change this, *because* — here is the reasoning, here is what could go wrong" *teaches*. The "because" is what makes review a mentorship channel rather than a list of orders. The author should come away understanding the *principle*, so they apply it themselves next time, unprompted.

*Distinguish the levels of a comment.* Not every comment carries equal weight, and a good reviewer makes the weight clear: this is a *blocking* issue that must be fixed; this is a genuine *suggestion* worth considering; this is a *minor preference, take it or leave it*; this is just a *question*, I am trying to understand. Some teams label these explicitly ("nit:", "question:", "blocking:"). Lumping a trivial preference and a serious bug into the same flat list of "comments" leaves the author unable to prioritize and makes a small review feel like a wall of demands.

*Be generous with praise.* A review is not only a list of things to fix. When the code is good — a clean solution, a well-handled edge case, a clear name — *say so*. Praise in review is not empty politeness; it tells the author *what to keep doing*, it makes the review a balanced human exchange rather than pure criticism, and it makes the inevitable critical comments far easier to receive.

*Right-size the review.* Reviewing well requires being able to *actually review* — and a giant change of thousands of lines cannot be reviewed well by anyone; the reviewer skims and rubber-stamps. Part of a healthy review culture is *small changes*, which are reviewable with real attention. (This connects to Chapter 21 — small changes are also safer to deploy; the same discipline serves both.)

*Review promptly.* A change waiting on review is an engineer *blocked* — and a reviewer who lets reviews sit for days is, however good their eventual comments, a drag on the whole team's flow. Timely review is part of doing the job well.

The summary: a good review is *technically rigorous* on the things that matter, and *humanly generous* in its delivery — kind, specific, explanatory, honest, prioritized. Done that way, every review the engineer gives makes the author measurably better and the team measurably stronger, which is leverage, which is the point.

## 28.4 How to Receive a Review

The other side of review is a skill too, and it is one engineers are rarely taught: how to *receive* a review well.

The foundational reframe: **a review is a gift, not an attack.** A colleague spent their time and attention to read your work and help make it better. That is generous, and the critical comments — the ones that sting slightly — are the *most* valuable part of the gift, because they are the ones that will make you better. An engineer who receives review *defensively* — who argues every point, takes every comment as a personal slight, treats the reviewer as an adversary — is *throwing away the gift*, and is also, in time, teaching colleagues that reviewing their work is unpleasant, which means they will get *less* and *shallower* review, which harms them further. So: receive review with **professional non-defensiveness**. Read each comment as what it is — an attempt to help — and engage with the *substance*, not the ego-hit. *Separate yourself from the code*: the code having a flaw does not mean *you* are flawed; all code, including the best engineers', has flaws a fresh reader will find, and that is *exactly why review exists*. Be *grateful for the catch* — a reviewer who found a real bug just saved you from shipping it, and that deserves thanks, not resentment.

This does not mean *agreeing with everything*. A reviewer can be *wrong*, or can be missing context the author has, and disagreeing with a review comment is entirely legitimate — but *how* you disagree matters: you disagree by *explaining your reasoning*, calmly, with the same collaborative spirit you would want in the comment itself, treating it as a *technical discussion* aimed at the best answer (Chapter 27's collaborative truth-seeking) rather than a *defense*. Sometimes that discussion will change the reviewer's mind; sometimes it will change yours; both are fine outcomes, and an author who is *visibly willing to be convinced* makes review a genuine conversation rather than a negotiation.

A staff-level note: how an engineer *receives* review is *watched*, especially by junior engineers, and it sets a norm. A senior engineer who receives review graciously — who thanks reviewers, engages openly, says "good catch," changes their mind when shown a better way — *teaches the whole team* that this is how it is done, and makes review *safe* for everyone. A senior engineer who receives review defensively teaches the opposite, and poisons the practice. Receiving review well is, itself, an act of leadership.

## 28.5 Mentorship: The Deliberate Development of Others

Beyond the constant, woven-in teaching of code review lies the more deliberate practice of **mentorship** — actively, intentionally helping another engineer *grow*. A staff engineer mentors, because mentorship is leverage in its purest and most compounding form, and because — as Section 28.7 will argue — *developing other* *engineers is part of the job*, not a favor done on the side.

First, a distinction Chapter 26 introduced and that is worth sharpening here: **mentorship and sponsorship are different, and both matter.** A *mentor* gives *advice and guidance* — they help the mentee think through problems, they share experience, they teach, they answer "how should I approach this?" A *sponsor* spends *their own credibility on the mentee's behalf* — they advocate for the mentee in rooms the mentee is not in, they put the mentee forward for the visible and growth-making project, they name the mentee in the promotion discussion and vouch for them. Mentorship helps an engineer *get better*; sponsorship helps an engineer *get* *recognized and get opportunities*. Both are needed, and a staff engineer should be conscious of doing *both* — and conscious that sponsorship, in particular, is something one can *only* do once one has credibility to spend, which makes it a specific responsibility of the senior engineer.

How to actually *mentor* well — because "be a mentor" is a vague instruction and the substance matters:

*Mentorship is led by the mentee's needs, not the mentor's agenda.* The mentee has their *own* goals, their *own* growth areas, their *own* situation — and good mentorship starts by *understanding those* and serving them, not by the mentor projecting the path that worked for *them*. The first work of mentoring is *listening*. *Teach by enabling, not by doing.* The constant temptation, when a mentee is stuck, is to *take over* and solve it — it is faster, it is satisfying, and it *teaches the mentee nothing*. Good mentorship resists that: it asks questions that lead the mentee toward the answer, it lets the mentee *struggle productively* (struggle is how skill forms), it offers the *minimum* help that unblocks. The goal is the mentee's *growing capability*, not the immediate solution, and those two goals genuinely conflict in the moment — a mentor who always optimizes for the immediate solution produces a mentee who stays dependent.

*Give honest, specific, actionable feedback — including the hard kind.* The most valuable feedback is often the hardest to give: the honest word about a real weakness, a blind spot, a habit holding the mentee back. Withholding it to be "nice" is not kindness — it deprives the mentee of exactly the information they most need to grow. Honest feedback, delivered with genuine care and made *specific and actionable* (not "be better at design" but "here, concretely, is the pattern I see and here is what to practice"), is one of the greatest things a mentor gives.

*Create stretch, and provide safety.* Growth happens at the edge of current ability — so a mentor helps create *stretch* opportunities, work slightly beyond the mentee's current proven range. And because stretch means *risk* *of failure*, the mentor provides *safety*: backs the mentee up, frames the inevitable stumbles as learning, ensures a failure is survivable. Stretch without safety is just risk; safety without stretch is stagnation; mentorship is the deliberate provision of both.

*Share the unwritten things.* Much of what a junior engineer needs is *not* technical and *not* written down anywhere — how the organization actually works, how to navigate a particular situation, what the unspoken expectations of the next level are, how to handle a hard conversation. A mentor who has this tacit knowledge and *shares* it gives the mentee something they could get almost nowhere else.

## 28.6 Growing a Team, Not Just Individuals

Mentorship one-on-one is powerful; a staff engineer also thinks about the growth of the *whole team*, because team-level development is leverage at a still larger scale.

This means caring about, and contributing to, the *conditions* under which everyone on a team grows. **Psychological safety** — Chapter 23's theme, recurring — is foundational: a team where it is *safe* to ask a question, admit a gap, make a mistake, and say "I don't understand" is a team where everyone *learns*, because learning requires exposing what you do not yet know; a team where those things are unsafe is a team where everyone hides their gaps and therefore *stops growing*. A staff engineer protects psychological safety actively — by modeling it (asking the "obvious" question themselves, admitting their own mistakes openly), and by ensuring that no one is punished or mocked for the vulnerability that learning requires.

It means treating **knowledge-sharing** as part of the team's normal operation — the design docs and reviews already discussed, but also: tech talks, pairing, documentation, deliberately *spreading* expertise so the team has no single points of human failure (the bus-factor problem). A staff engineer notices when knowledge is concentrated dangerously in one person and works to spread it. It means *modeling* the standards rather than only stating them — because a team learns most from what its senior people *visibly do*. A staff engineer who writes excellent tests, receives review graciously, communicates clearly, and admits mistakes openly *teaches the whole team those things* by demonstration, continuously, whether or not they ever give a single piece of explicit advice.

And it means caring about the team's *culture* — the norms, the safety, the way people treat each other — as a thing a staff engineer can *shape* and is partly responsible for, not a fixed environment they merely inhabit.

## 28.7 The Principle: Make More Senior Engineers

The chapter closes on the principle that elevates everything in it from "nice things to do" to "core responsibility": **a staff or principal engineer's job explicitly includes developing other engineers — including developing them toward staff and principal.** Multiplying others is not a generous extra a staff engineer does *if there is time*. It *is* the job, or a central part of it, because staff-level impact *is* impact through others, and the most lasting impact through others is *making them more capable*.

There is a generational, compounding logic here that is worth seeing clearly. An engineer who reaches staff or principal and *hoards* — who does the impressive work themselves, who guards knowledge, who does not mentor, who is the indispensable single point of expertise — has a *ceiling* on their impact: it is bounded by their own capacity, and it *ends* when they leave. An engineer who reaches staff or principal and *develops others* — who mentors deliberately, sponsors generously, teaches through every review, spreads knowledge, raises the whole team — has impact that is *unbounded* and that *outlives them*: the engineers they developed go on to do excellent work, and to develop *others*, and the improvement *compounds across the generations of a team and* *an organization*. The mentor's mentees become mentors. The standards spread. A principal engineer who, over a career, helped a dozen engineers reach staff has had *vastly* more impact than one who, over the same career, personally built twice as many systems — because the dozen engineers are still going, and still multiplying, long after.

This also reframes the security an engineer might feel from being *indispensable*. The instinct to be the one-who-knows, the bottleneck everything routes through, can feel like job security. It is, in truth, a *limitation* — on the engineer (who can never step away, never grow past that role) and on the team (which is fragile, single-point-of-failure'd around them). The genuinely senior move, the confident move, is the opposite: to *make* *oneself dispensable* — to spread one's knowledge so thoroughly, and develop one's colleagues so well, that the team would be *fine* without you. That is not a threat to a staff engineer's standing; it is the *proof* of staff-level work, because it means the engineer's impact has successfully transferred into *other people* and *the system*, which is exactly what staff-level impact *is*. The engineer who has made themselves dispensable in their current role is, not coincidentally, the engineer who is *free* to take on the next, larger problem — and *that* is how a career compounds.

So the staff engineer measures themselves, in part, by a question that has nothing to do with their own commits: *who have I made better, and how much, and are any of them on a path to where I am?* An organization full of engineers who ask themselves that question is an organization that gets relentlessly stronger. Making more senior engineers is not charity. It is the highest-leverage, longest-lasting, most genuinely senior work there is.

> ### War Story — The Reviewer Who Made Everyone Worse
>
> *A team had an engineer who was, technically, very strong — and who was known, throughout the team, as* *a harsh reviewer. Their reviews were thorough — they caught real things, and the things they caught were* *genuine — but they were delivered with no care for the human on the other end. The comments were blunt* *to the point of contempt: terse, often phrased in a way that implied the author was careless or slow, heavy* *with the reviewer's evident impatience, and they critiqued the person as readily as the code — "this is* *wrong," "you clearly didn't think about X," "why would you do it this way." There was no praise, ever. There* *was no explanation of the why — just verdicts. Every comment, trivial or serious, arrived at the same flat* *level of stern disapproval, so a misplaced space and a real design flaw felt equally like indictments. The* *reviewer would have said, if asked, that they were simply holding a high bar, and that the content of their* *reviews was correct — and the content largely was correct.*
>
> *And the effect on the team was severe and entirely invisible in any individual review. Engineers came to* *dread having this person review their work. The newer engineers dreaded it most — a harsh review from a* *senior engineer, early in your time on a team, is genuinely demoralizing — and they began, rationally, to* *adapt: they avoided having that engineer review their changes when they could, routing around them; when* *they could not, they made the smallest, safest, least ambitious changes they could, because a bold change* *meant a longer, harsher review. They stopped asking the reviewer questions. They stopped learning from* *the reviews entirely — because a review received in a state of dread and defensiveness teaches nothing;* *the author's whole attention is on surviving the comments, not absorbing them. The reviewer's genuine* *technical knowledge — which was real, and which could have lifted the whole team — reached no one,* *because the delivery had made it impossible to receive. Worse, the harshness was contagious in reverse: it* *taught the team that review was a thing to fear and to minimize, and so the team's overall review culture* *grew thinner and more defensive, and the team, collectively, got worse — less bold, less open, learning less* *from each other — and a meaningful part of the cause was one technically-excellent engineer whose* *reviews were correct and cruel.*
>
> *A staff engineer eventually had the direct conversation, and the framing of it is this chapter's lesson. The* *problem was not the reviewer's standards, which were fine, and not the technical content of their reviews,* *which was accurate. The problem was that the reviewer had fundamentally misunderstood what code review* *was for. They had treated it as a bug-catching gate — and as a pure bug-catching gate their reviews were* *arguably effective. But code review is a teaching, knowledge-spreading, team-building practice that also* *catches bugs, and judged as that — judged on whether it made the authors better and the team stronger —* *the reviewer's reviews were not merely failing, they were actively destructive: they were making engineers* *more timid, less communicative, and less able to learn, and they were degrading the team's whole culture of* *review. A review that is technically correct and humanly cruel is not a good review with a rough edge; it is,* *measured by code review's actual purposes, a bad review, because it produces the opposite of what code* *review exists to produce.*
>
> *The reviewer, to their credit, heard it, and changed — learned to explain the why, to distinguish a nit from a* *blocker, to critique the code and not the person, to give praise when praise was earned, to write from the* *assumption of good intent. Their reviews stayed just as technically rigorous — they caught just as many real* *problems — but now the rigor landed, because it was wrapped in something an author could receive. And* *the team, over time, recovered: engineers stopped routing around them, started learning from them, started* *being bolder again. The lesson is the chapter's: code review's purpose is to multiply the people around you,* *and a review that is correct but cruel does not multiply — it diminishes. The technical content of a review is* *necessary; it is the humanity of the delivery that determines whether that content makes the team better or* *makes it afraid.*
>
> ### The Level Line — Code Review and Mentorship
>
> **A senior engineer*** gives technically sound code reviews, receives review professionally, and helps junior* *engineers when asked.*
>
> **A staff engineer*** understands code review as a teaching, knowledge-spreading, team-building practice and* *reviews accordingly — rigorous on what matters, humanly generous in delivery, explaining the why,* *distinguishing nits from blockers, praising real quality; receives review as a gift and models that for the team;* *mentors deliberately, led by the mentee's needs, teaching by enabling rather than doing, giving honest hard* *feedback; sponsors as well as mentors; and protects the psychological safety the whole team's growth* *depends on.*
>
> **A principal engineer*** treats developing other engineers — toward staff and principal — as a core, explicit* *part of the job; builds the team's whole culture of review, learning, and safety; spreads knowledge so the* *team has no single points of human failure; makes themselves dispensable as a sign of work done well; and* *measures their own success substantially by who they have made better and how far those people have* *gone.*
>
## 28.8 At the Interview Table

Code review and mentorship are probed directly in behavioral and leadership rounds — heavily so at staff and principal level, because they are direct evidence of the impact-through-others that defines those levels.

**The question: "How do you approach code review?"**

A weak answer is "I look for bugs and style issues." A strong answer shows the full understanding: "I think of code review as a teaching, knowledge-spreading, team-building practice that *also* catches bugs — if it were only bug-catching, automated tools would do much of it more cheaply. So when I review, I prioritize: correctness and design first, then clarity and tests and security, and style last — and style I'd rather have tooling enforce so human attention goes to what needs judgment. And I care as much about *how* I deliver it as *what* I find: I assume good intent, critique the code and not the person, always explain the *why* so the comment teaches a principle rather than just ordering a change, distinguish blocking issues from suggestions from nits so the author can prioritize, and give genuine praise when the work is good. The goal of every review is that the author comes away better, not just that the change comes out cleaner."

**The question: "Tell me about a time you mentored someone." / "How do you develop other engineers?"** These directly probe the impact-through-others that staff level requires. A strong answer shows *deliberate* development: starting from the mentee's *own* goals and needs rather than a generic program; *teaching by* *enabling* — "I resisted just solving it for them, and instead asked questions that led them to it, because the goal was their growing capability, not the immediate fix"; giving *honest, specific* feedback including the hard kind; creating *stretch* with *safety*. And ideally it distinguishes mentoring from *sponsoring* — "I also made sure to advocate for them: I put them forward for a visible project and spoke up for them in planning, because mentorship helps someone get better but sponsorship helps them get *recognized*." An answer that shows someone was genuinely, measurably grown — and especially one where the mentee was later promoted — is exactly what the question wants.

**The question: "How do you handle receiving critical feedback?"**

"I try to treat a review or critical feedback as a *gift* — someone spent their attention to help me improve, and the critical comments are the most valuable part, because they're what make me better. I separate myself from the code — all code has flaws a fresh reader finds, including mine, and that's exactly why review exists, so a flaw found isn't a judgment on me. I engage with the substance, not the ego-hit, and I'm grateful for a real catch. If I genuinely disagree, I'll say so — but by explaining my reasoning calmly, as a technical discussion aimed at the best answer, and I stay genuinely open to being the one who's wrong. And I'm conscious that *how* I receive review is watched, especially by junior engineers — so receiving it graciously is also a way of making the practice safe for the whole team."

**The question: "What does it mean to have impact beyond your own work?"** (the Chapter 26 question, with a Chapter 28 answer)

"A large part of it is *multiplying* the people around me. If I make ten engineers each meaningfully better — through code review that teaches, through deliberate mentorship, through spreading knowledge — that's more total impact than anything I could build alone, and it *compounds*, because those engineers carry the improvement forward and pass it on. I think a senior engineer's job explicitly includes developing other engineers, including toward staff — and a sign of doing it well is actually making yourself *dispensable* in your current role: spreading your knowledge so thoroughly the team would be fine without you, which both proves the impact transferred and frees you for the next larger problem."

**The red flags.** Viewing code review as only bug-catching. Harsh, person-directed, or unexplained review with no awareness of its human effect. Receiving feedback defensively. Treating mentorship as a favor rather than part of the job. Mentoring by *doing it for* people. Hoarding knowledge or valuing indispensability. And, at staff/principal level, a candidate whose entire story of impact is personal output with no mention of growing others — which signals they have not understood what the level *is*.

## 28.9 The Forge

**Drill 28.1.** Take a real code change (yours or a sample, ideally one with both genuine problems and genuine strengths) and write a complete review of it: prioritized correctly (design and correctness before style), with the *why* explained on every substantive comment, with nits clearly distinguished from blockers, and with genuine praise where the code is good. Then re-read your review as if you were the author and assess how it would *feel* to receive.

**Drill 28.2.** Take a blunt, harsh, person-directed review comment ("this is wrong, you didn't think about X") and rewrite it three times: as a clear blocking comment that explains the why and assumes good intent; as a suggestion; as a question. Notice how much the same underlying point changes in its effect.

**Build 28.1.** Mentor someone, for real, over a period of time — a junior colleague, a student, someone learning. Practice the chapter's principles deliberately: start from *their* goals, teach by enabling rather than doing, give at least one piece of honest hard feedback, create a stretch opportunity with safety. Afterward, write up honestly what you did well, what you did poorly, and what you learned about mentoring.

**Build 28.2.** Identify a piece of knowledge that is concentrated dangerously in one person on a team you know (including possibly yourself) — a bus-factor risk. Make a concrete plan to *spread* it: documentation, a tech talk, pairing, a deliberate handoff. Where possible, actually execute it.

**Investigate 28.1.** Reflect honestly on how *you* receive code review. Recall a recent review where you felt defensive — and examine, honestly, whether the defensiveness was warranted or was ego protecting itself. Then, on your next several reviews received, deliberately practice treating each as a gift, and write up what changed.

**Investigate 28.2.** Study how reviewing is done on a healthy open-source project — read a number of real review threads. Identify what the good reviewers do: how they prioritize, how they explain, how they distinguish severity, how they handle disagreement, how authors and reviewers treat each other. Write up what makes the culture work.

**Design 28.1.** You are a staff engineer on a team whose review and growth culture is unhealthy: reviews are harsh and demoralizing (or, alternatively, are shallow rubber-stamps), junior engineers are afraid to ask questions, knowledge is concentrated in a couple of people, and there is no real mentorship happening. Write a three-to-four-page plan to transform it: how you fix the review culture (the standards for giving and receiving, the role of tooling, the human norms); how you build psychological safety; how you spread concentrated knowledge; how you establish real mentorship and sponsorship; and how you would model all of it yourself. This is the staff-level exercise — building a team that multiplies its people, which is building a team that compounds.
